i.Summary

Most organisations learn how a process really works by talking about it in workshops, handovers and process-discovery calls. Writing those conversations up as a proper process map takes an analyst hours, and it often misses that two teams described the process differently.

Transcript to BPMN takes the recordings or transcripts of those meetings and drafts the process map automatically, in BPMN 2.0, the standard notation used by process tools such as Camunda and Signavio. It does three things a plain AI summary does not:

  • Every step can be traced to the words behind it. Each box on the map links to the quotes it came from, with the speaker, the meeting and the timestamp.
  • Several meetings become one map. When a second team describes the same process, their account is merged into the existing map rather than producing a second, competing one.
  • Disagreements are shown, not hidden. Where meetings contradict each other (who does a step, what the approval threshold is, which order things happen in), the tool lists each point and lets a person settle it with one click.

People stay in charge. The map can be edited by hand or by typing a change in plain English, and every change is kept as a revision that can be undone. The result can be exported as a BPMN file, a diagram image, or a written standard operating procedure (SOP).

What this proof of concept shows

The whole journey works end to end in a working web application: upload meetings, get a draft map, merge a second meeting, review the disagreements, settle them, edit, and export. It runs with organisation-level data separation, retention rules and an activity log.

What it does not show yet

We have not yet measured how accurately the AI drafts maps from real meetings. The examples in this report use reference ("ideal") readings of two short sample meetings in place of a live AI model, so they show what the product does with a good reading, not how often the AI gets the reading right. Measuring that is the first step of the plan in Section VII, and it needs AI-model access and 15–20 real meetings.

What we're asking for

  1. Approval of an AI provider for the evaluation (OpenAI, or Azure OpenAI if data must stay in the company's cloud tenancy).
  2. 15–20 real process meetings, each with a process map the business already trusts, so accuracy can be measured honestly.
  3. One pilot team (for example accounts payable or HR onboarding) to use it for four weeks once accuracy is acceptable.

ii.The problem and our approach

Why process mapping is slow

Process knowledge mostly lives in people's heads, and it comes out in conversation. A typical mapping exercise looks like this:

  • An analyst runs several interviews or workshops with the teams involved.
  • They re-listen to recordings or reread notes and draw the map by hand.
  • Contradictions between teams ("finance approves over $5,000" versus "over $10,000") are found late, often only when the map is reviewed, or not at all.
  • Nobody can easily see why a step is on the map, so reviewers have to trust the analyst's reading.

The meetings are already happening and are increasingly recorded and transcribed by Teams, Zoom or Meet. The missing piece is turning those transcripts into a map people can trust.

How it works for the user

i.Add meetingsDrop in a transcript (Teams, Zoom or Meet export, Word or text) or a recording (audio or video). Processing starts on its own.
ii.Get a draftThe tool sets aside off-topic chatter, identifies the steps, who does them, the decisions and their conditions, and draws the map.
iii.Settle differencesEach new meeting is merged into the process's single map. Contradictions become numbered points to settle.
iv.Refine and exportEdit visually or in plain English, undo any revision, and export as BPMN, an image, or a written SOP.

Principles behind the design

PrincipleWhat it means in practice
Evidence for every stepEvery step, decision and handover keeps a link to the transcript lines it came from. Reviewers check the source instead of trusting the tool.
AI proposes, people decideThe tool never quietly picks a side when meetings disagree. It asks. Edits are versioned and reversible.
The AI never writes the file directlyThe AI only describes the process in a structured form. Ordinary, predictable software checks that description (no dead ends, no steps without an owner) and produces the BPMN file, which is checked against the official BPMN 2.0 standard before anyone sees it.
Standard outputMaps are standard BPMN 2.0, so they can go into the organisation's existing process tools rather than being locked into this one.
Meeting content is sensitiveEach organisation's data is kept separate, content can be deleted automatically after a set number of days, and the activity log records who did what without recording what was said.

iii.Worked example: supplier invoice approval

This example uses two short sample meetings written for testing. They are deliberately small so the whole journey fits on a few pages, and they contain the kind of contradictions that real teams produce.

Meeting one: accounts payable and finance

A supplier invoice lands in the AP inbox, and someone on my team, um, enters it into the ERP. Then we match it against the purchase order. If it doesn't match, we send it back to the supplier.Priya · 0:06
And if it matches, anything over five thousand dollars comes to me in finance for approval.Marco · 0:20
Once it's approved either way, the payment run picks it up on Friday and pays the supplier.Marco · 0:33

From this meeting alone the tool draws the following map. It puts each step in the team that does it (the horizontal bands, called lanes) and shows the two decisions: does the invoice match the purchase order, and is it above the $5,000 threshold.

Meeting two: finance and treasury

A week later, a different group describes the same process:

If it matches and it's over ten thousand dollars, Marco approves it. Ten thousand or less, we approve it in AP.Sana
After approval, treasury validates the supplier's bank details.Marco
And then treasury pays the supplier in the Friday payment run.Sana

The tool recognises that "AP keys each invoice into the ERP" is the same step as "enters it into the ERP", even though the wording differs, so it does not add a duplicate. It adds the step that is genuinely new (treasury validating bank details) and the new Treasury team. It finds three points where the meetings disagree:

  1. i.Who pays the supplier? Meeting one implies Finance; meeting two says Treasury.
  2. ii.When does finance approve? Over $5,000, or over $10,000?
  3. iii.When does AP approve directly? $5,000 or less, or $10,000 or less?

Reviewing the disagreements

The process page lists each point in plain English, with the two answers as buttons. The same numeral marks the affected step on the diagram, so a reviewer can see exactly what is in dispute and click through to the quotes from both meetings before choosing.

The settled map

Here the reviewer confirmed that Treasury pays suppliers and that the threshold is $10,000. Each choice was applied to the map as a new revision, so any of them can be undone. The final map has three teams, the bank-details check, and the corrected thresholds:

Settling the thresholds also updated the decision itself: it now reads "Amount over $10,000?", so the diagram, its branches and the written procedure all agree.

Without the tool, an analyst would have had to spot all three contradictions by comparing notes from both sessions. Here they were listed automatically, with the evidence for each side attached.

iv.The workspace and what it produces

The proof of concept is a complete web application, not a script. It is designed to feel like a well-kept case file rather than a dashboard: an index of processes, a file for each one, and an editor.

Editing

  • Visually: drag, add, rename or delete steps in the diagram editor.
  • In plain English: type a change such as "Finance also checks the VAT number before approving" and the tool applies it to the map. The change is checked before it is saved, and a change that would break the map is refused.
  • Safely: every save, AI edit and settled point is a numbered revision that can be undone. If two people edit the same map at once, the second person is told the map has changed and nothing is silently overwritten.

Outputs

ExportUse
BPMN 2.0 fileImport into process tools such as Camunda or Signavio. Each file is checked against the official BPMN 2.0 standard before it is offered.
Written procedure (SOP)A readable document listing the roles, numbered steps with owners, the decisions and their conditions, and the outcomes. Each step quotes the meeting lines it came from.
Diagram image (PNG / SVG)For slides, wikis and reports. Drawn by the same component as the editor, so it looks exactly like the editor.

An excerpt of the SOP exported for the settled invoice process, unedited apart from shortening.

Supplier invoice approval

Standard operating procedure generated from meeting transcripts. Quotes show where each step came from; review before publishing.

Roles: Accounts Payable · Finance · Treasury    Starts when: Invoice received

  1. Enter invoice into ERP, Accounts Payable
    On our side, AP keys each invoice into the ERP and checks it against the purchase order.Sana · transcript_2.txt
  2. Match invoice to purchase order, Accounts Payable

Decision (Accounts Payable): Invoice matches PO? If no match: go to step 3 (Return invoice to supplier). If it matches: go to the amount check.

Decision (Accounts Payable): Amount over $10,000? If over $10,000: go to step 5 (Approve invoice (finance)). If $10,000 or less: go to step 4 (Approve invoice (AP)).

  1. Validate supplier bank details, Treasury
    After approval, treasury validates the supplier's bank details.Marco · transcript_2.txt
  2. Pay supplier in weekly payment run, Treasury
    Once it's approved either way, the payment run picks it up on Friday and pays the supplier.Marco · transcript_1.vtt · 0:33

Note that step 7 cites both meetings: the procedure shows that the step was confirmed by two separate groups, which is useful evidence when the SOP is reviewed or audited.

v.Data, privacy and control

Meeting recordings can contain sensitive information, so these controls are built into the proof of concept rather than left for later:

ControlWhat it does
Separate workspacesEach organisation's processes, meetings and maps are kept apart, and this is enforced on every request, not just hidden in the interface.
Automatic deletionEach process can be set to delete its recordings and transcript text after a chosen number of days. The maps stay usable; their quotes then show as "no longer available".
Full deletionDeleting a process removes its recordings, transcripts, maps and revisions permanently, after a type-to-confirm step.
Activity logRecords who uploaded, edited, settled, exported or deleted what, and when. It never records what was said in a meeting.
Access keysAccess is by keys issued per person or integration. Each can be revoked at once, and only a scrambled (hashed) form of each key is stored.
Usage limitsLimits on requests and monthly processing per organisation keep costs predictable.
Choice of AI providerThe AI provider is a configuration setting. Switching to Azure OpenAI, so that data stays inside the company's own cloud tenancy, requires no code changes.

Where meeting data goes. Transcripts are sent to the configured AI provider to be read; recordings are sent to a transcription service when no transcript is available. Nothing else leaves the system. Which provider is acceptable is a decision for the business and its data-protection lead (see the asks in Section I).

vi.Evidence: what has and hasn't been measured

We have tried to be precise about what this proof of concept proves. It is useful to separate the system into two parts: reading the meeting (the AI's job: deciding what the steps, owners and decisions are) and everything after that (merging, finding disagreements, checking, drawing, editing, exporting), which is ordinary, predictable software.

vii.Current limitations and the road ahead

Limitations today

  • AI accuracy is unproven on real meetings. This is the main open question (Section VI).
  • English only. Other languages have not been tried.
  • Sign-in is by access key. There are no individual user accounts, roles or company single sign-on (SSO) yet, and no billing.
  • Not yet deployed for others to use. It runs in a local environment and has not been load-tested or security-reviewed by a third party.
  • Messy real conversations (people talking over each other, processes described out of order, long tangents) are untested. The two sample meetings are short and orderly.
  • Recordings rely on an external transcription service, and how well speakers are told apart depends on that service. Platform transcripts from Teams, Zoom or Meet remain the better input.

A.Appendix: glossary and supported formats

TermMeaning
BPMN 2.0Business Process Model and Notation: the international standard for drawing business processes, readable by most process-management tools.
Process mapA diagram of the steps in a process, who does them, and the decisions that change the path.
LaneA horizontal band on the map for one team or role. Each step sits in the lane of whoever does it.
Decision (gateway)A diamond on the map where the path splits depending on a condition, for example "Amount over $10,000?".
SOPStandard operating procedure: a written, step-by-step description of how a process is carried out.
Point of disagreementA place where two meetings describe the process differently. The tool lists it for a person to settle rather than choosing for them.
RevisionA saved state of the map. Every edit and settled point creates a new revision, and any revision can be undone.
RetentionHow long meeting recordings and transcripts are kept before being deleted automatically.
